Introduction

Who was {{ page.title }}?

Best known as Lilith in modern culture, the name has roots in Sumerian, Akkadian, and Hebrew. Lilu is Sumerian for “wind demons”; Lilitu is Akkadian for “female demons.” Combined with the Hebrew word Lilit, or “night creature,” these elements gave birth to Lilith.3 Lilu refers to a group of demons that could manipulate storms, and Lilitu is a demon that could fly like a bird. Another origin for Lilith is a group of restless spirits known as the Ardat-lili, believed to be the ghosts of young women who died unwed and without children. These spirits roamed the wilderness and haunted the night, seeking men to seduce by creeping through windows into their homes.4 They preyed on men, women, and children alike. Inscriptions on incantation bowls say Lilitu seduced young men in their sleep. Other bowls describe her as haunting men in their dreams and being blamed for their nocturnal emissions.5 Lilitu caused illness in pregnant women. One bowl describes Lilitu as having a reputation for roasting their victims, usually children and infants.6 According to one inscription, “evil Lilith, who causes the hearts of men to go astray, and appears in the dream of the night and in the vision of the day, who burns and casts down with nightmare, attacks and kills children, boys and girls.”7

Lilitu’s earliest appearance comes from a poem titled Gilgamesh and the Huluppu Tree. In the poem, the goddess Inanna plants a huluppu tree in her sacred grove at Uruk and waits for it to grow into a throne and bed. Three intruders settle in the tree: a snake “which fears no spell” in the roots, Lilitu in the trunk, and the Anzu bird in the branches. Gilgamesh comes with his bronze axe and kills the snake; the Anzu bird flies to the mountains; and Lilitu tears down her house and flees into the desert.8 The story sets up a pattern that Lilitu follows for the next few thousand years: a female demon/intruder in a domestic space that must be driven out into the wilderness.

Lilitu exists to explain the unexplainable. In a civilization that depended heavily on children to ensure its continuation, demons like Lilitu often posed a serious threat to Mesopotamians’ way of life. Other civilizations, such as the Babylonians and Assyrians, believed that pregnant women were most vulnerable to demonic attacks.9 Lilitu was also used to explain nightmares, sexual dreams that men could not control, and unexplained illness. Because they had limited knowledge of modern medicine or the reproductive system, they turned to the only rational explanation they could: a demon must be behind this.10 The demons Lilu, Lilitu, and Ardat-lili appear multiple times across several demon lists and incantations, including the Udug-hul, along with other major demons such as Lamashtu and Labasu.11 They also appear in the anti-witchcraft series known as the Maqlu lists.12 Lilith appears often in different manuscripts and magical texts, while little to no information exists on her male counterpart, the gurus-lil-la. Some scholars suggest this reveals a pattern in ancient magic: female demons were more widely known and feared than male demons.13 Demons like Lilitu entered through windows and thresholds, so these entry points became the focus of protective sealing and deposit rituals.14 Because of their association with unmarried, childless women who died, these demons served as a cultural warning to women who were not married or did not have children.15

02
Artifacts

What she left behind

Thousands of ceramic bowls dating from roughly 400 to 800 A.D. have been unearthed across Iraq and neighboring regions. They are ordinary household ware, and their inner surfaces bear spells, prayers, and ritual instructions for protection against demons.16 Lilith frequently appears on these bowls as a figure with unruly hair, wings, and exaggerated feminine features, shackled or bound in rope, underscoring her seductive nature.17

  • Bowers Incantation Bowl

    Excavated from a building foundation at Babylon in 1947, it bears Aramaic inscriptions that reference Lilitu.18 The text and illustrations suggest that women also performed rituals for protection, demon expulsion, and healing. These bowls portray them as “antagonists of the demons.”19

    Image courtesy of Bowers Museum.

  • The Queen of the Night

    A baked-clay plaque depicting a nude figure with feminine features, wings, and bird-like talons, standing on lions with owls on either side. Raphael Patai argues that the plaque depicts Lilitu.20 Other scholars have noted that the figure wears a multi-horned crown of divinity and carries the ring-and-rod symbol of power, leading some to believe it may depict the goddess Inanna.21

03
Rituals

How She Was Expelled

When it came to expelling Lilitu, exorcists used several methods.

Incantations and Commands

  1. i.
    Calling Her by Name Incantations addressed her and other demons by name: “Lili or Lilitu… depart from before me, go out of the house!”23
  2. ii.
    Invoking Marduk Another canonical text has the exorcist identify himself with Marduk to overpower her: “I am the captor, the hero Marduk who weakens the captive Lilu-demon, … who weakens the captive Lilith, … who weakens the captive Ardat Lili.”24

Fire and Purification

  1. iii.
    The Maqlu ceremony The Maqlu ceremony is a night-long ritual in which the exorcist raises a torch and burns statues and effigies made in the likeness of demons such as Lilu, Lilitu, and Ardat-lili. This ceremony took place at the end of Abu (late July/August), when the veil between this world and the netherworld was at its thinnest, allowing spirits to cross over.25
  2. iv.
    Censers, Torches, and Fumes Udug-hul rituals usually involved passing censers and torches over the possessed victim, throwing holy water and purifying oils on hot coals, and letting the fumes fill the home.26

Protecting the Home

  1. v.
    Sealing Doors and Windows They used substances like pure fat and gypsum on doors and windows to prevent demons from entering the home, much like Western methods of keeping evil spirits out, such as placing pieces of iron at doorways.27 Additionally, they recited incantations like “witchcraft, sorcery, magic, evil rites not cross the threshold of the house,” and that good spirits may come and bless the house.28
  2. vi.
    Figurines to Lure Her The exorcist also used two kinds of figurines. One was typically made in the likeness of Lilitu or a related entity like “maiden of lilu-demon,” and the exorcist placed them around the possessed victim. The goal was to get the demon to attach itself to one of the figures.29
  3. vii.
    Figurines to Guard Homes The other was two figures wrestling with each other, placed on the threshold of the house. It served as a message to uninvited guests that: “This house is occupied by fighters.”30

Turning Demons on Witches

Witches had the power to summon demons to their bidding, such as to torment their victims.31 So, if someone was under demonic attack, the question was “who sent them.” The appropriate solution was to fight fire with fire: turn the demons on the witch by using this incantation: “Lilu, Lilitu, Ardat-lili you have caused to seize me: May Lilu, Lilitu, Ardat-lili seize you.”32

Concluding Remarks

Although she went by different names and the rituals and talismans changed from figurines to incantation bowls to amulets, the core elements (her name, binding her, keeping her out of homes, keeping her away from children) that gave rise to the modern depiction of the demon known as Lilith remained stable for over 3000 years. This is why scholars have treated the Lilith of Jewish folklore and the Lilitu of Mesopotamian mythology as a single, evolving figure rather than as two separate entities.33
