Introduction

Understanding Mesopotamian Beliefs

In ancient Mesopotamia, the gods and religion were at the center of everyday life. It was the way they attempted to understand the world around them by believing there were higher powers at work that dictated how things happened. They would build temples and dedicate cities to these higher-powered beings1 because they believed that the gods could bring misfortune and destruction if they were angered.2 Their belief in this higher order of deities also gave rise to supernatural evil beings that would cause terrible things to happen and bring misfortune.3 These beliefs were important as they show how the Mesopotamians viewed misfortune and allow us to see how they attempted to protect themselves from dangers that they simply could not explain with the knowledge that they had.4 So, being able to study the negative beings, or demons, helps provide a better understanding of how their supernatural beliefs affect everyday life.

Although gods and demons were both supernatural forces in the Mesopotamian supernatural world, the distinction between them was a little more complicated than just a "good versus evil" relationship.5 One difference was their respective positions within the divine hierarchy of Mesopotamian culture. The gods held recognized positions within the pantheon that affected the political and social structures of Mesopotamian cities.6 This hierarchy included different levels of authority and responsibilities, closely resembling human society. In light of their positions, gods were more easily communicated with by humans through prayer, hymns,7 and offerings.8 This differs from the position that demons held in Mesopotamian society, as they were understood to exist outside of this hierarchy. Jonathan Beltz explains in his dissertation "Namtar: Deity, Demon, Agent of Fate" that the distinction between gods and demons depended partly on how humans interacted with them.9 While people could appeal to the gods for assistance,10 the demons were commonly dealt with through protective rituals, incantations, or exorcisms.11 With this distinction, demons in ancient Mesopotamia were not simply evil gods, but separate entities that required different responses from Mesopotamian society.12

The categories of gods and demons were not always clearly separated.13 Some beings possessed supernatural characteristics that allowed them to fit into multiple categories, depending on the context in which they were presented.14 One example discussed by Beltz is a being named Namtar, who was typically associated with the underworld, death, disease, and fate.15 Namtar held a position in the court of Ereshkigal, the goddess of the underworld, but was also associated with harmful forces that could be seen as demonic in certain Mesopotamian texts.16 This makes Namtar an important example of the flexibility of roles in Mesopotamian religion, demonstrating how supernatural beings did not always fit into clearly defined categories. Beltz explains that Namtar could be understood not only as a member of the divine hierarchy, but also as a being associated with demonic practices.17 This example shows the importance of understanding Mesopotamian demons within their own religious context instead of putting them in a clearly defined role.

Demons had a significant influence on Mesopotamians' understanding of the world around them, especially its negative aspects, such as illness or death.18 Rather than viewing these unfortunate incidents as natural events, Mesopotamians understood them as the result of supernatural forces.19 This suggests that demons were not simply characters in myths or stories20 but were a way for ancient Mesopotamians to explain why harmful events happened.21 As a result, Mesopotamians had a reason to seek protection and assistance through religious practices.22 Understanding the role demons played in Mesopotamian life helps us understand the relationship between humans and the supernatural world in Mesopotamia.

As a result of their belief in the harmful effects demons had on human well-being, ancient Mesopotamians developed various practices in order to protect themselves from these negative forces.23 These incantations and rituals were used in response to situations viewed as supernatural dangers to remove harmful influences.24 Exorcistic practices were important as they served as a way to confront these beings that were believed to cause harm. In Babylonian Witchcraft Literature: Case Studies, Tzvi Abusch explores texts connected to incantations, rituals, and witchcraft that were intended to address these supernatural happenings. His research highlights the importance of written magical and therapeutic traditions that ancient Mesopotamians used in response to these threats.25 These practices show how Mesopotamians actively responded to supernatural dangers rather than simply believing that demons existed. Rather than simply combating demons, ancient Mesopotamians developed practices to help protect themselves from these harmful forces.26 Through these practices, religious beliefs became connected to Mesopotamians' everyday concerns about health and safety.

Overall, demons were an important part of ancient Mesopotamian religion and society as a whole. It was believed that demons were connected to dangers people experienced or feared in their everyday lives.27 These beliefs influenced how Mesopotamians viewed and understood topics such as illness, death, or misfortune. The difference between gods and demons may also reveal how Mesopotamians organized the supernatural world and how they could interact with it. By examining the roles that demons played in Mesopotamian society and the threats they were believed to pose, we can better understand the relationship between religion and everyday life in ancient Mesopotamia. Ultimately, examining these beliefs allows us to see how the supernatural was connected to the way Mesopotamians viewed and understood their world.

Secondary Sources

Discussion of Secondary Sources

The Outlier: Namtar

In Beltz's dissertation titled "Namtar," the entity Namtar is portrayed as both an underworld deity and a demon who causes plagues and illnesses, underscoring his dual nature and resistance to easy categorization.28 He shared power over disease with other malevolent spirits and even certain gods, such as Erra. Scholars are still trying to determine how his role in disease has changed over time. As a god of the underworld, exorcists invoked Namtar to drag offenders, such as witches, down to the underworld. Like Namtar, Pazuzu was a kind of "king" who had power over lesser demons. His power and authority came from his role as king of the wind demons. Although he was a demon who caused suffering, protective objects bearing his likeness were used to ward off other demons. However, one key difference is that an incantation references Pazuzu casting out Namtar, which may suggest that Namtar was a lesser being than Pazuzu.29

The Duality of Demons

According to Gina Konstantopoulos's article "Demons and Exorcism in Ancient Mesopotamia," demons did not have fixed natures; instead, people judged them by their actions.30 For example, the Udug-hul references the demonic Sebettu, who raged at not receiving offerings despite evidence of temples built in their honor; the kubu demon, an entity representing stillborn children, spread disease but also appeared in personal names as protection against evil.31 This duality went both ways: Babylonian incantations commanded evil spirits to leave while calling on good spirits to tend to the victim. The word "demon" comes from the Greek daimon ("spirit" or "divine power"), which was neither strictly good nor evil. Exorcists adapted incantations originally designed to ward off demons, using them against witches and even invoking demons to turn them against witches. For example, a witch could call upon Lamashtu, Labasu, Lilu, and Lilitu and send them to attack their chosen victim, while the exorcist could use a counter-incantation to turn the demons against the witch.32

The Protectors Against Demons

The āšipu, or exorcist, was the best defense against demonic attacks. His authority over demons derived from studying the appropriate texts and the gods.33 He began every ritual by following the "script," which depicted an interaction between Marduk, the god of exorcisms, and Enki, the god of wisdom, on how to help a possessed victim. Marduk handed down the ritual instructions to the exorcist, giving the gods a way to officiate the ritual.34 The responsibility for detecting illness lay with the exorcist and the gods, not the victim, while the ritual relied on the victim's acceptance that demonic attacks could be countered by benevolent spirits. Before performing the ritual, the exorcist took preventive measures to protect himself from demonic attacks. The Udug-hul instructs him to grind nikiptu plants into honey and ghee, then anoint himself with the mixture before approaching the victim.35 Other protective measures included using non-human staff, such as figurines of demonic protectors, which were planted or buried at weak points in the house, such as beneath windows, in doorways, and in corners. All of these were considered potential entry points for demonic infestation. Even demons could be called upon to help the exorcist; for example, figurines modeled after Pazuzu were used against demons like Lamashtu.36 Another object used to ward off or trap evil spirits was the incantation bowl. In Nippur during the 1880s, thousands of incantation bowls were discovered in nearly every excavated house. The bowls were buried face down at potential entry points, such as corners and thresholds. Text inscribed on the rims and interiors of the bowls contained formulas, prayers, biblical passages, and incantations for expelling evil spirits. Lilith appeared frequently on these bowls as a figure with unruly hair and wings, bound in shackles or rope.37

David Frankfurter's article titled "Scorpion/Demon: On the Origin of the Mesopotamian Apotropaic Bowl" suggests that incantation bowls may have originated as traps for another kind of pest.38 The article notes that people already used inverted tableware to trap scorpions, a very real and lethal danger. It raises the question of whether these incantation bowls were the ancient equivalent of roach motels. Other methods for warding off demons include the zissura (boundary marker), zidubdub flour, a gišhur-magic circle drawn around the bed, a bronze ball, the urigal reed standard, a scapegoat (figurine), and a censer and a torch.39 The exorcist drew the circle around the bed to protect the most vulnerable space, the victim's sleeping body. This circle, like the incantation bowls, trapped demons.

When it came to ailments such as physical and mental illness, they were often treated as signs of demonic attacks. The Udug-hul series served primarily as an instruction manual for treating illnesses, which were viewed as the random, uncontrolled activity of demons and ghosts.40 The classifications linking demons to illnesses were very specific. For example, Asakku was associated with epilepsy, headaches, jaundice, chills, di'u disease, and huruppu disease.41 Other spirits, such as ghosts, were associated with head and neck pain, tinnitus, stomach issues, chest and breathing problems, fever, and neurological and mental disorders. There were two ways to present demons as harmful entities: first, demons were treated as external attackers that "seize" or "strike" their victims. Second, demons could cause harm inside their victim's body, which was then flushed out like water or drawn out like poison. In the second view, for example, rituals for dealing with the demon Namtar involved rubbing bread on the victim. This method was meant to draw the demon out of the victim, like drawing out poison. Diagnostic texts such as the Sagig assigned each symptom pattern to a responsible agent and a prognosis.42 Medical prescriptions went further into detail about which spirit caused which symptom. For example, if a man kept seeing dead people, he was being tormented by the ghost of someone who was killed by a weapon and left in the steppe.43 When it came to mental illness, the Udug-hul texts portrayed patients as the "distraught man," someone who suffered from psychic distress and angst, constantly walking because he could not rest. The distraught man's pain was both physical and mental, with the two overlapping and manifesting as symptoms such as epilepsy, anxiety, arthritis, and depression.44

Organization

How This Project Is Organized

Structure of the Website

Our group decided to organize the website by focusing on individual Mesopotamian demons. We chose this approach because each demon has distinct characteristics, beliefs, and roles within Mesopotamian society. Studying them separately allows us to examine each demon in depth and identify connections and differences among them. We also decided to include a separate overview page about exorcists because of their relevance to Mesopotamian demons. Finally, the website will include a complete bibliography of the sources used throughout our research.

Layout of Each Demon Page
