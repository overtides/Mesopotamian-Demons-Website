Introduction

Why Did {{ page.title }} Exist?

In Mesopotamian culture, people acknowledged a spiritual world of gods, demons, and other entities. They believed these entities, especially demons, influenced daily life. Demons were semi-divine beings who were often overlooked compared to the gods, who had lineages, temples, and stories to glorify them. They were often associated with desolate places like deserts and had power only over the earthly realm. Whenever someone was afflicted by illness, experienced misfortune, or died prematurely, the most logical explanation was that a demon was responsible.1 Some demons were supposedly so elusive that they even evaded detection by the gods themselves: “unknown both in heaven and earth, concealed by an aura, not even recognized among the wise gods.”2 Medical texts reference demons like Lamastu and other evil spirits, linking them to physical ailments as well as mental disorders and treating them as signs of demonic oppression or possession.3

02
Qualifications

The Education of an Exorcist

According to tradition, the most common and effective way to expel demons was to perform an exorcism. As in other religious traditions such as Christianity, only a qualified individual can perform the ritual. Mesopotamians relied on an āšipu, an expert healer or incantation priest.4 To be an āšipu, one had to study and master two texts: The Exorcist’s Manual and the Udug-hul. The Exorcist’s Manual includes rituals such as brick-god incantations, mouth-washing rituals to purify divine statues, investiture texts, washing rituals, the great purification series Bit rimki and Bit meseri, and rituals for specific months and for the king’s cult.5 The Udug-hul is a series of sixteen tablets, each containing incantations against demons. One could consider the Udug-hul to be an ancient precursor to grimoires.6

03
Rituals

How Were Demons Expelled?

Protective Measures Before Rituals Began

To perform the ritual, the exorcist had to prepare himself. Before an exorcism, the exorcist recites a chant because something may go wrong, such as the ritual attracting the demon he is trying to banish or drawing unwanted attention from other entities: “May they not approach my body… nor enter my house, nor clamber up to my roof.”7 Most exorcisms began with the exorcist telling the same story about the gods Marduk-Ea and Asalluhi-Enki. Marduk, the god of exorcisms, notices that someone is afflicted by a demon and goes to his father Enki, the god of wisdom. Marduk tells his father that he does not know what to do. Enki reassures his son: “What I know, you also know.” He then tells him, “Go, my son Marduk!” Marduk then hands over the ritual instructions to the āšipu, fixing the problem. The exorcist recites the dialogue himself, playing Marduk. Telling this story was the exorcist’s way of winning the gods' favor and making the ritual legitimate.8

The exorcist's ultimate goal was to restore balance by banishing the demon, ensuring it stays out, refilling the emptied space with protective good, and leaving the victim and house clean and guilt-free, restoring their standing with the gods.

In addition to the texts, the exorcist used other tools and talismans to help banish demons.

Purification Rituals

Purification was essential in exorcisms to keep evil spirits at bay and was often associated with water and fire.

  1. i.
    The River Water Purification The exorcist would take a vessel, fill it with water from the Tigris and Euphrates, add tamarisk and mastakal herbs, recite the Eridu spell over it, sprinkle it over the possessed victim, and then pass the censer and torch over him.9 The Udug-hul describes the exorcist cleansing one victim “7 times 7 times” with pure river water.10
  2. ii.
    The Burning E'ru-Wood Rod The exorcist also used an e’ru-wood rod. He set fire to the tip and base of the rod and kept the flame over the victim’s head day and night to keep demons at bay.11
  3. iii.
    The Removal of Impurity Another cleansing method involved wiping the victim with flour and then throwing it into the fire. This was more to benefit the victim’s psychological well-being by relieving him of any guilt he may be carrying.12

Removing and Containing Demons

As in other approaches to exorcisms, it was not enough to cast the demon out because it could always return or jump into another host. To prevent the demon from returning, it either had to be sent back from whence it came or transferred into another vessel. According to myth, Marduk asked to “introduce a substitute (figurine)” that could contain or trap the demon.13

  1. iv.
    Figurines for Demonic Extraction In one ritual, a black figurine was tied near the patient’s head and laid on his body to draw the spirit out; afterward, the exorcist placed seven figures at the victim’s head and two “wrestling” figures on the threshold. The wrestling figures warned demons, “This house is occupied by powerful spirits,” to keep them from invading the home.14
  2. ii.
    Anti-Witchcraft Rituals Rituals where the exorcist would burn wax or clay figurines modeled after demons and witches.15
  3. iii.
    Trapping Demons Preventive magic and exorcisms also blended into objects such as incantation bowls, which were buried upside down in the corners of houses to trap demons already inside and keep others out.16

Incantations and Blessings

Incantations and chants also played a significant role in the exorcist’s battle with evil spirits. The most effective way to expel demons was to command them to leave in the gods’ names. In other words, the exorcist invoked the gods' power to banish demons.17 Each chant was forceful and repetitive: “Be off, be off, begone, begone! Depart, depart, flee, flee! From my body be off! … To my body turn back not!”18 Some chants dramatized the ritual: “Whether you are the evil Alu-demon who prowls about quietly at night like an urban fox… I am the incantation priest and purifier of the rites of Eridu.”19 Once the demon is driven out, the exorcist may close by calling upon a blessing on the victim and the house: “May the good spirit and good genius be present at his side, may they not cease.”20 Here, these chants appear crucial for the exorcist to assert his power and authority over these spirits.
