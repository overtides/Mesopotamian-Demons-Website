Introduction

Who was {{ page.title }}?

Pazuzu was an ancient Mesopotamian demon associated with destructive winds which caused sickness and storms, and was considered the king of the "evil wind-demons."5 Pazuzu was not regarded solely as a demon: his inscription identifies him as “the god Pazuzu,” indicating that he could also be understood as a deity within Mesopotamian religious beliefs.6 Pazuzu originated around 1000 BCE, and was associated with opposing Lamashtu. Pazuzu was not a protective spirit who lent his aid to the āšipu. Instead, incantations involving Pazuzu were told from Pazuzu's point of view.7 Unlike benevolent forces, Pazuzu's nature was not altered in his incantations: his destructive nature itself is directed against Lamashtu.8 His protection against Lamashtu is ultimately a side effect.9 Additionally, Pazuzu had power over lesser demons, such as Namtar, and the lil-spirits.10 Pazuzu was also associated with the cold winds that blew from the Zagros Mountains, which were believed to bring illness, and a gatekeeper of the netherworld.11

Rituals

Using his power

Amulets and Protective Objects

  1. i.
    Pazuzu-Head Amulets Pazuzu-head amulets were worn as protective objects, especially by pregnant women, to ward off Lamashtu and protect unborn children.30
  2. ii.
    Bronze Pendants Bronze Pazuzu pendants provided portable protection: some had suspension loops and could be worn, while others carried protective inscriptions.31
  3. iii.
    Inscribed Pazuzu Amulets Some amulets included cuneiform inscriptions invoking Pazuzu’s protective power in addition to using his image.32

Figurines and Protective Plaques

  1. iv.
    Bronze Figurines as Household Protection Bronze figurines of Pazuzu could be used as household protective objects. For example, the Louvre’s MNB 467 statuette contains an inscription identifying Pazuzu as the king of the evil wind demons.33
  2. v.
    Protective Plaques Against Lamashtu Protective plaques depicted Pazuzu confronting or driving away Lamashtu, representing his power to defend people against her attacks.34

Incantations

  1. vi.
    Standard Pazuzu Incantation A This incantation presents Pazuzu speaking in the first person, identifying himself as the son of Hanbi and king of the evil wind demons.35
  2. vii.
    Standard Pazuzu Incantation B This incantation addresses Pazuzu directly and invokes his power. These incantations were inscribed on Pazuzu-head amulets, combining written spells with protective imagery.36
  3. viii.
    Incantations Against Other Harmful Spirits and Illness Pazuzu was invoked against destructive winds and other harmful supernatural forces. Incantations called upon his power to keep these dangers away from individuals and households.37

Important Secondary Sources

How Scholars Understand {{ page.title }}

1. Constance E. Gane — Composite Beings in Neo-Babylonian Art

Gane examines composite (hybrid) beings in Neo-Babylonian art and explains how their physical features, symbolism, and roles reflected Mesopotamian beliefs. Her research provides important context for understanding how supernatural beings could serve protective purposes despite their frightening appearances. This is relevant to Pazuzu because, while considered a dangerous demon, he could also be used for protection through amulets and figurines.38

2. Eckart Frahm — "A Tale of Two Lands and Two Thousand Years: The Origins of Pazuzu"

Frahm examines the origins and historical development of Pazuzu in Mesopotamian culture, discussing his significance as a supernatural being. He explains how Pazuzu’s association with destructive winds and his authority over other wind demons also gave him a protective role, particularly in defending households against harmful supernatural forces. Frahm's research provides important context for understanding Pazuzu's dual role as both a terrifying demon and as a protective figure.39

3. Frans A. M. Wiggermann — "The Four Winds and the Origins of Pazuzu"

Wiggermann examines the origins of Pazuzu and his relationship with the four winds in Mesopotamian demonology. He explains how Pazuzu's association with destructive winds and authority over other wind demons contributed to his protective role. This source is relevant because it helps explain how Pazuzu could serve as both a dangerous demon and a protective figure.40

Primary Source Analysis

Case Study: Pazuzu Demon Figurine (MNB 467)

The bronze figurine of Pazuzu is catalogued as MNB 467 at the Louvre, is an example of how Mesopotamians understood protective supernatural powers. It is worth noting that bronze was a valuable material in Mesopotamia because producing bronze ingots required copper and tin, which were not readily available locally. The use of bronze in this figurine reflects the metalworking resources and craftsmanship involved in producing protective objects in Mesopotamia. Additionally, bronze was used to create divine images and objects associated with the gods, highlighting the significance of using this material for a figurine of Pazuzu.41

Pazuzu is shown as a hybrid being, combining human and animal visual characteristics. Its inscription reads: "I am Pazuzu, son of Hanbi, king of the evil wind demons. I ascend the mighty mountains, making the winds rage. It is I." This inscription establishes Pazuzu's identity and his authority over destructive winds and other supernatural forces and connects Pazuzu with mountains and the wilderness. His description as king of the evil wind demons reflects the association between supernatural beings and dangerous, unsettled places. The reference to raging winds emphasizes his destructive tendencies. However, his authority over these forces helps explain how a terrifying demon could also serve a protective purpose when invoked against other harmful beings.

Pazuzu's protective role was closely connected to his power over other harmful spirits, especially Lamashtu. Lamashtu was believed to threaten pregnant women, mothers, and infants, making childbirth and early childhood important areas of supernatural concern. Protective objects depicting Pazuzu were used to defend against such threats. Gane discusses the possible relationship between the use of protective objects and concerns about maternal and infant mortality, emphasizing that many children did not survive early childhood.42 Therefore the use of these protective objects could stem from the desire to protect mothers and children from Lamashtu. Although the inscription on MNB 467 does not specify pregnant women, infants, or Lamashtu, it does identify Pazuzu as a powerful figure who commands dangerous supernatural forces. This association helps explain why people might seek his protection against harmful spirits. Thus the figurine illustrates how Pazuzu could represent both destructive and protective powers.

The use of Pazuzu figurines and amulets also demonstrates how beliefs about demons influenced everyday life. These protective objects provided people with a way to seek supernatural protection for themselves and their families. Pazuzu’s role thus illustrates the importance of religious beliefs and protective practices in responding to the dangers of pregnancy, childbirth, infancy, and household life.
